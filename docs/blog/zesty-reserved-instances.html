Automating Reserved Instance management with Zesty

Using less and paying less per unit are two different parts of FinOps. Our Zesty integration handled the second one, commitment management, with Terraform deciding which accounts could be observed and where managed actions were allowed.

Why commitments needed an operating process

An infrastructure team can spend a lot of time tuning workloads while commitment decisions stay an occasional spreadsheet exercise. That creates its own kind of drift. The instance mix changes, testing environments sleep, teams resize applications, and a reservation that matched yesterday's baseline may not match today's.

Zesty was part of how we automated Reserved Instance management. The interesting engineering story isn't that a vendor could buy a reservation. It's how we connected the vendor to our organization, kept observation separate from managed access, and kept commitment decisions tied to changing infrastructure demand.

I'd look at this alongside scheduled sleep, node provisioning, and rightsizing. A discount on capacity you don't need isn't as good as getting rid of that capacity. Commitment planning should start from the demand that's left after those changes.

How the integration was wired

The organization module created a read-only Zesty role whenever the integration was enabled. A separate condition turned on the managed role. Cost and Usage Report access had its own configuration path. The linked-account modules also kept read-only access apart from an optional write-access role.

The trust policy let a vendor principal assume the role under an ExternalId condition. I've left out the real principal and external identifier on purpose. They're configuration and security context, not useful article material.

The organization's managed policy included EC2 Reserved Instance actions: buying offerings, creating or canceling listings, modifying reservations, and requesting or accepting exchange quotes. The read-only policy covered discovery across EC2, organizational context, cost data, and Savings Plans information.

That difference matters. Savings Plans read actions in an IAM policy don't mean the integration was allowed to buy Savings Plans. The write policy I looked at supports the Reserved Instance story. A newer product capability and its permission needs would get their own review.

Read-only first is a useful boundary

Onboarding read-only first lets the team look at account coverage, usage history, existing commitments, and the vendor's recommendations before turning on any purchasing. It's also a good way to confirm the vendor is seeing the right organization and billing scope.

Zesty's current documentation describes Commitment Manager as agentless, built on an IAM integration. It also lists Cost and Usage Reports and Reserved Instance Marketplace registration as requirements for that workflow. Those are today's product prerequisites. They don't mean a host agent was running in our clusters. Zesty integration requirements.

Splitting the roles in Terraform shows a deliberate permission boundary, but it doesn't make the setup least privilege by itself. The linked-account write policy also included operational actions on instances, load balancers, snapshots, and volumes, and some of those can delete or stop resources. They deserve a separate review from permission to manage discount commitments.

I'd map each permission to the product feature that needs it, and avoid granting a broad write policy just because the first use case is buying reservations. Review organizational purchasing scope, linked-account operating scope, report access, trust conditions, and any encryption permissions one by one.

A reservation is not a running instance

EC2 Reserved Instances are a billing benefit applied to eligible usage under specific matching rules. They shouldn't show up in a Kubernetes diagram as another worker node. AWS also treats regional and zonal reservations differently, so discount planning and capacity planning both need to keep the real product scope in mind. AWS Reserved Instance overview.

For our platform, that means the commitment workflow and the node provisioning workflow have different jobs. Karpenter picks a node shape to fit a pod. Whether a reservation applies depends on whether the resulting usage matches the commitment. Buying a discount doesn't guarantee a GPU instance will be available when a job needs one.

I'd document the eligible family, region, platform, term, and flexibility of each commitment type. The automation needs those matching rules as much as it needs a trend line. A planned move between instance families, or less overnight demand, can change the baseline that's actually useful.

Utilization and coverage answer different questions

Utilization is how much of the commitment you used. Coverage is how much eligible usage got the benefit. You need both. High utilization can sit next to low coverage, and high coverage can sit next to a commitment that's bigger than what you still need.

The reconstructed view uses made-up normalized cost units on one common basis. A commitment of 0.60 units per hour with 0.54 consumed has 90% utilization and 0.06 unused. If eligible usage is 1.00 units per hour on the same basis, consumed coverage is 54%.

Consumed units cannot exceed either the commitment or eligible demand. Zero denominators have no ratio. This example performs no purchasing action and predicts no savings or resale outcome.

The common basis is on purpose. A teaching ratio shouldn't divide a discounted commitment cost by some unrelated on-demand-equivalent total and call it an exact AWS coverage metric. For a real report, use the billing dataset's defined utilization and coverage measures, and be explicit about their dimensions and how fees are treated.

How I would roll out managed actions

The Terraform kept managed access behind its own switch. I'd keep that boundary and add a concrete review package before flipping it: observed usage, existing commitments, eligible baseline, planned infrastructure changes, permission scope, and an accountable owner from both finance and engineering.

Start with a defined account and product scope. Review which purchase or exchange actions are allowed, the vendor configuration, and the reporting cadence. Make sure the people who own budgets understand the term and the remaining financial obligation. A fleet that keeps changing is a reason to revisit the baseline, not a reason to chase maximum coverage automatically.

The operating review should cover seasonal changes, expected migrations, instance-family changes, and workload shutdown schedules. Our move to workload-driven capacity made this especially relevant: a static reservation plan could fall behind a pool that now chooses from many more instance shapes.

Nothing in this article or its interactive example buys a reservation. The configuration I describe comes from the integration we ran, and the rollout sequence is the review process I'd use when setting it up or revisiting it.

Monitoring the result and the automation

I'd keep commitment performance separate from automation health. Performance means utilization, uncovered eligible usage, unused obligations, term expiry, and changes in the matched fleet. Health means failed role assumptions, report delivery problems, failed managed actions, and unexpected policy changes.

Review purchases and modifications through the cloud audit trail. Reconcile vendor reporting with the Cost and Usage Report and with whatever billing view finance uses. Include amortization, fees, credits, and refunds where your reporting definition calls for them. A lower EC2 line item alone doesn't mean the whole change came from one vendor or one project.

In operational Grafana views, I'd show clearly defined summary metrics and link to the billing evidence. A missing report is an unknown, not zero unused commitment. When an instance family or region changes, that should visibly trigger a review.

Turning off managed write access stops new authorized actions. It doesn't undo a commitment already bought, cancel its remaining term, or guarantee you can sell it on the marketplace. Recovery has to deal with the vendor integration and the remaining obligations separately.

The lesson I would carry forward

Automating commitment management meant we stopped treating recurring analysis as a one-off task. Terraform gave the integration a permission structure people could review. The discipline that mattered was keeping the automation tied to billing evidence, workload changes, and a clear owner.

I'm still not going to put a savings percentage on this. What I have shows the integration and its permission model, not a full study of what we'd have spent otherwise. The stronger claim is that commitment management became an infrastructure workflow with visible boundaries, instead of an occasional purchase disconnected from the platform.
