Application maturity that asks for evidence

A deployment can succeed while the service is still hard to operate. Our application maturity model made that gap visible, with a questionnaire per domain and the weakest score deciding what to improve next.

Why a shared readiness model was useful

Teams mean different things when they say an application is production-ready. One is thinking about automated deployment. Another is thinking about redundancy, access control, a restore procedure, or whether anyone knows who gets the alert.

That ambiguity causes real problems. A platform team can't prioritize support well if a critical application and an experiment look the same in the registry. An application team can't plan reliability work if the expectations are scattered across chat threads. And during an incident, finding out that ownership and recovery are unclear is very expensive.

Our maturity guide gave everyone a common language for these discussions. Applications lived in a registry, with questionnaires covering several domains. The overall score was the lowest domain score. If any questionnaire was incomplete, the overall result was zero.

That's the part I like most. It forces an uncomfortable but useful question: what's the weakest part of running this service? A great deployment pipeline doesn't make up for a restore path nobody has tested.

Below I walk through the model as we documented it and how I'd make its reviews useful today. The example domains and review screens are generalized. They aren't a real assessment of any particular production application.

Read the levels as operating expectations

The model ran from M0 to M4. The progression went from experimentation, through an operating foundation, toward resilience and automation. Some of the numerical targets in the original document were policy choices for that time. They shouldn't be copied into every service as universal requirements.

LevelMain questionExample evidence
M0Are we still learning what the service should do?Named owner, experiment scope, and an explicit limit on customer exposure
M1Can a limited group use it with known constraints?Controlled rollout, basic monitoring, documented limitations, and a rollback route
M2Can a team support ordinary production operation?Repeatable delivery, quality and security checks, health signals, recovery instructions, and appropriate availability design
M3Can it tolerate expected failures and changing demand?Dependency failure tests, safe deployment behavior, capacity evidence, and tested recovery
M4Can well-understood failures be handled automatically?Bounded remediation with auditability, continuous performance validation, and evidence of safe automated decisions

M4 was an aspiration in our guide; its checklists were never implemented. I won't claim we reached automatic remediation just because the level was written down.

The levels should help a team choose its next piece of work. They shouldn't turn into badges that reward writing longer answers, or punish a deliberately small internal service for not looking like a customer payment API.

The weakest domain determines the next conversation

A readiness review should finish with evidence

Follow the review from service ownership to one verifiable improvement.

Define the service

Record the customer journey, criticality, owner and operating scope. An experiment and a payment API have different exposure decisions.

owner + criticality + dependencies

Say a service scores three for operations, two for security, three for delivery, and two for recovery. The overall result is M2. An average would give 2.5 and hide exactly the gaps that matter when the service fails.

An incomplete questionnaire isn't the same as a low score with evidence behind it. The rule gives zero when any questionnaire is incomplete. That zero means the assessment isn't finished. It shouldn't be read as proof that the application has no useful controls.

Taking the minimum is deliberately conservative, and it has limits. Two services can both be M2 with very different risks. A team needs to see the domain scores, the missing evidence, customer exposure, and how critical the service is, alongside the headline level.

Ask questions that can be verified

A questionnaire gets useful when each answer points to evidence. "We have backups" is weak. "We restored the latest backup into an isolated environment, checked these records, measured this recovery time, and wrote down the remaining gap" gives another engineer something to evaluate.

For an operating foundation, I'd ask just a few questions:

  • Who owns the service, and who responds outside normal working hours?
  • Which customer journey does it support, and which dependencies can prevent that journey from completing?
  • Can someone deploy and roll back using the documented workflow?
  • What detects a bad deployment or a missing stream of telemetry?
  • What has actually been tested about restoring data and rebuilding the service?

Security and delivery deserve the same treatment. A configured scan isn't a reviewed finding. A green pipeline isn't a tested upgrade of an application with real data. A runbook that exists isn't the same as another engineer successfully following it.

The review should link to test results, dashboards, change records, and runbooks, with enough context to understand what each one covers. Links expire or point at old versions, so every piece of evidence needs a date and an owner.

Keep the registry small enough to maintain

We kept the application registry and questionnaires in Jira. That was practical: a review could link straight to the work needed to close a gap. A separate portal would only have helped if it made that link easier to maintain.

A generalized record could look like this:

yaml
application: payments-api
owner: payments-team
criticality: customer-facing
review:
  date: 2026-10-02
  domains:
    operations: 3
    security: 2
    delivery: 3
    recovery: 2
  overall: 2
  evidence:
    rollback: "Reviewed deployment test record"
    restore: "Restore drill still required"
  next_action:
    description: "Run and record an isolated restore drill"
    owner: payments-team

This is an illustrative data model, not our Jira schema. The fields that matter are the ones you need to make a decision: owner, criticality, domain results, evidence, review date, and the next action. Collecting dozens of fields nobody maintains would only weaken the registry.

Some facts can be collected automatically, like whether a service has an owner annotation or a deployment workflow. Automation can check that a control exists. It can't tell from a config flag that recovery works or that a person understands an alert.

Reliability targets need their own reasoning

The original document attached availability percentages and latency targets to each level. They tell you what we were aiming for at the time, but I'd rethink how a maturity score connects to a service promise.

A service-level indicator measures behavior, like the share of valid requests that succeed. A service-level objective sets a target for it over a defined window. A service-level agreement is an external commitment, possibly with consequences. Maturity is an assessment of operating capability. They're related, but they mean different things.

An internal reporting tool can be mature because its ownership, delivery, security, and recovery are appropriate and tested, even though it doesn't need a customer API's availability objective. And putting a 99.99% label on a service doesn't give it the architecture, telemetry, or response process to actually meet it.

I'd choose objectives from the user journey and business needs, then use the maturity review to ask whether the team can measure and operate against them. The Pyrra article explains why the measurement window and the history you actually keep matter here.

Turn a low score into a bounded improvement

A review meeting should end with a next step that has an owner and can be checked. "Improve reliability" is too vague. "Test losing one dependency, record what users see, and add a bounded timeout if the request hangs" is something an engineer can ship and someone else can review.

For the example M2 application, the next recovery step might be an isolated restore drill. The delivery side might already be strong; asking that team to improve deployment again would be less useful than fixing the weakest domain.

I'd keep improvement plans small: the main gap, the risk it creates, the change needed, who's responsible, and what evidence closes it. If a team accepts a risk for now, write down its scope and a review date. Permanent, unexplained exceptions slowly make the whole model meaningless.

Reviews also need a trigger to happen again. A dependency change, a data migration, a new owner, or a big jump in customer exposure can all make an earlier assessment wrong. Waiting for the calendar review can leave the registry describing a service that's no longer the same.

Introduce the model without making it a gatekeeper

I'd start with two very different applications: a small internal service and an important customer journey. Go through the questions with their owners, find the confusing wording, and compare the evidence each one needs. This is how I'd roll it out now, not a record of how the organization adopted it.

Next, publish worked examples of an incomplete review and an evidenced M2 review, and explain the difference between missing answers and missing controls. Feed the resulting improvements into normal team planning instead of a separate backlog nobody looks at.

The platform team should provide paved paths: deployment templates, owner labels, dashboard examples, runbooks, and an easy way to test recovery. If every team has to build the same foundation on its own, the maturity model just points out the duplication without fixing it.

Finally, check whether the model is helping. Are ownership questions answered faster? Do restore tests produce usable evidence? Do teams know which gap to tackle next? I'd measure those before claiming the model improved uptime. I don't have a measured drop in incidents to quote.

What I would keep and what I would revise

I'd keep the shared vocabulary, the per-domain view, and the conservative minimum rule. They make hidden operational gaps much easier to talk about.

I'd drop universal numerical targets, separate an unfinished assessment from a proven gap, and keep M4 clearly aspirational until its controls are defined and tested. I'd also make evidence freshness part of the review, because an answer that was right once can mislead after the service changes.

The real value is a clearer relationship between the platform and the teams using it. The model says what supportable operation looks like, the platform makes it easier to get there, and the application owner brings the evidence.
